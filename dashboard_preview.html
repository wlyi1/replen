How Replenishment Is Calculated

Every formula in the pipeline, in the order it runs — from raw sales history to the final ordered quantity.

1Mean Weekly Demand (μ)

μ = (1 ÷ N) × Σ dᵢ
The average number of units sold per week over the available sales history (N weeks). This is the baseline "typical week" every other demand formula builds on.

2Demand Std. Deviation (σ / std_qty)

σ = √[ (1 ÷ N) × Σ(dᵢ − μ)² ]
How much week-to-week demand swings around the average (population std. dev.). A SKU that sells a steady 10/week has a low σ; one that sells 0, 0, 40, 0 has a high σ even with the same average — and a high σ is exactly what drives a bigger safety buffer below.

3Coefficient of Variation & XYZ Class

CV = σ ÷ μ
Normalizes volatility relative to how big the average demand is, so a fast- and slow-mover can be compared fairly. This sorts every SKU into: X (steady, CV < 0.5), Y (moderate, 0.5 ≤ CV < 1.0), or Z (volatile/intermittent, CV ≥ 1.0 or undefined when μ = 0). Combined with the ABC revenue tier (A/B/C/D/E), this gives the two-letter class you see everywhere in the app (e.g. "AZ").

4Demand Forecast (forecast_qty)

X / Y classes — Simple Exponential Smoothing (α = 0.5):
forecast = α × latest_week + (1−α) × previous_forecast

Z class — Croston Method (intermittent demand):
forecast = avg(non-zero weeks) ÷ avg(weeks between non-zero sales)
The method is picked automatically by XYZ class. SES weighs recent weeks more than old ones — smooth, responsive, good for demand that sells most weeks. Croston is built specifically for items with lots of zero-sale weeks: it separates "how much when it does sell" from "how often it sells," which SES badly overstates or understates for intermittent SKUs.

Each ABC-XYZ combination maps to a target probability of not stocking out. Higher-value, more-predictable SKUs (AX) get a higher target service level — and therefore a higher Z — while low-value or highly volatile ones get a lower target. This Z feeds directly into the buffer stock formula next.

6Buffer Stock

Buffer Stock = ⌈ Z × σ × √L ⌉
The safety cushion held to absorb demand swings while waiting for a reorder to arrive. It scales with how volatile demand is (σ), how aggressively you want to avoid stockouts (Z), and how long you're exposed to that risk (√L — the standard square-root-of-time rule for combining independent weekly variance over L weeks of lead time). Result is rounded up (ceiling) since you can't hold a fraction of a unit as safety stock.

7Minimum & Maximum Stock

Minimum Stock = ⌈ Forecast + Buffer ⌉
Maximum Stock = ⌈ Minimum + Forecast ⌉ = ⌈ 2×Forecast + Buffer ⌉
Minimum is the reorder point — once effective stock falls to this level it's time to reorder, since it only covers expected lead-time demand plus the safety buffer. Maximum is the order-up-to level — replenishing up to Max covers roughly two forecast cycles plus buffer before you'd need to reorder again.

8Effective Qty & Target Selection

Effective Qty = Total Qty (on hand) + In-Transit Qty

Target Stock = Maximum Stock if replenish_mode = "max"
Target Stock = Minimum Stock if replenish_mode = "min"
Effective Qty is what you can actually count on soon — physical stock plus anything already shipped and en route. Whether a category replenishes up to Max or just up to Min is configurable per ABC-XYZ class / pharmacy in Control Center → Config (defaults to "max" everywhere).

10Unit Conversion (sales → box units)

Ordered Qty (boxes) = ⌈ Normal Qty ÷ Conversion Factor ⌉
Suppliers ship in boxes/cartons, not loose units, so the sales-unit shortage is rounded up to the nearest whole box — you'll never see an order for a fraction of a box. This is also why "Replenish Qty" (sales units) can look like it barely moves between two lead times even when Min/Max clearly changed: if the box count needed doesn't cross a ceiling boundary, the sales-unit-equivalent quantity stays identical.

11Planogram Target Gap

Effective Qty (boxes) = Effective Qty ÷ Conversion Factor
Target Gap = max( ⌈ Plano Unit Qty − Effective Qty (boxes) ⌉ , 0 )
The planogram specifies how many boxes should sit on the shelf, completely independent of the demand-forecast math above — a slow-moving SKU can still have a shelf-facing requirement. This is the exact formula behind the Plano Floor and Hold→Plano columns: it converts current stock into box units first (dividing by the conversion factor) before comparing against the planogram's box target, since the two numbers naturally look similar even when they're in different units and shouldn't be compared directly.
Example: plano wants 6 boxes, conversion factor is 40 (40 sales units per box), and the pharmacy has 6 sales units on hand. That's only 6÷40 = 0.15 of a box — nowhere near the 6-box target — so Target Gap = ⌈6 − 0.15⌉ = 6 boxes, not 0. "6 vs 6" looks like a match only if you forget one side is sales units and the other is boxes.

12Planogram Gap Override

Plano Floor: Ordered Qty = Target Gap if Target Gap > Ordered Qty (else unchanged — never decreases)
Hold→Plano: Ordered Qty = Target Gap always, for SKUs on the hold_to_plano.csv allowlist
Two related but separate mechanisms: the unconditional Plano Floor applies to any SKU on the planogram and can only raise the order quantity, never lower it. Hold→Plano is a curated allowlist that fully replaces the quantity with the planogram gap, overriding the demand-formula result entirely for that SKU. Both are skipped for rows already flagged "No Follow" / "Hold Product" / "Hold to Stock Out" — a planogram rule can't reactivate a SKU someone explicitly suppressed.

13Plano Stock Ratio Gate

Plano QTY (sales units) = Plano Unit Qty × Conversion Factor
Ratio = Effective Qty ÷ Plano QTY

If (Plano Floor applied OR Hold→Plano applied) AND Ratio ≥ 0.6:
  Ordered Qty = 0, Status = "No need to replenish"
Else: unchanged — proceeds to TMP allocation as normal
A third check stacked on top of §12, still before TMP allocation. Even when the planogram floor or the Hold→Plano allowlist says a SKU should replenish, this skips it anyway if the pharmacy already holds at least 60% of the planogram's own shelf target — there's no point competing for (possibly scarce) warehouse stock over a SKU that isn't meaningfully under-stocked relative to its own target. Only a ratio under 0.6 lets the planogram-driven quantity through to TMP allocation; everything from 0.6 up is suppressed to 0 regardless of what §11–12 computed. Rows neither planogram mechanism touched are unaffected by this gate.

14Hold Rule Overrides

Hold Product: Ordered Qty = 0
Hold to Stock Out: Ordered Qty = 0
Hold to Planogram: Ordered Qty = Target Gap (see §11–12)
Manual business rules from Control Center → Hold / Hold to Planogram, applied per SKU (optionally scoped to specific pharmacies) and take precedence over the formula-driven number: Hold Product pauses ordering entirely, Hold to Stock Out deliberately lets stock run down to zero without reordering, and Hold to Planogram forces strict shelf-target compliance regardless of what the demand math says.

15Warehouse (TMP) Allocation

If TMP Qty = 0: Stockout TMP, Ordered Qty Final = 0

Shareᵩ = Requested Qtyᵩ × (TMP Qty ÷ Σ Requested Qty)
Base Allocationᵩ = ⌊ Shareᵩ ⌋
Remaining boxes = TMP Qty − Σ Base Allocationᵩ
→ handed out 1 box at a time, highest fractional remainder first, lowest pharmacy ID breaks ties
Runs only when the central warehouse doesn't have enough stock to fully satisfy every requesting pharmacy at once. Each pharmacy's share is proportional to how much it asked for relative to total demand; whole boxes are handed out first, then any leftover boxes go to whichever pharmacies were closest to rounding up. This is the only step that depends on what every other pharmacy is asking for at the same time — which is why editing one pharmacy's Lead Time in the dashboard recalculates instantly (formula-only) without re-running this allocation across the other 24.

16Flagged SKU Threshold

Total Position = Replenish Qty + Effective Qty
L7D Ratio = Total Position ÷ L7D Sales Qty

Red (overstock risk): L7D Ratio > 1.30 (position exceeds L7D sales by 30%+)
Yellow (underestimate/stockout risk): L7D Ratio < 0.30
Green: 0.30 ≤ L7D Ratio ≤ 1.30

L30D Flag (red only) = (Total Position ÷ L30D Sales Qty) > 1.00
Not "is this order big" on its own — it's "will this order plus what's already on hand sit well above, or well below, recent sell-through." Red: a SKU that just replenished a lot but also sells fast won't flag; one with a modest order but very little recent demand will. Yellow is the opposite risk: position barely covers 30% of last week's sales, which can mean demand was underestimated and this SKU may stock out before the next cycle. Both are sanity-check signals, not necessarily errors. The L30D flag only has the red (over) side — no yellow equivalent has been added for it.
